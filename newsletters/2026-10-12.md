# AI News — Monday, October 12, 2026

> A day after Satya Nadella said every model should be treated as compromised, Microsoft's president went further and asked governments for binding rules: incident reports, a duty to tell victims, and outside sign-off before release. A quieter story from last week makes a useful contrast to OpenAI's proof dump: Google DeepMind's maths agent got its open-problem results through peer review in Science, with every proof machine-checked.

## Top Stories

### [Microsoft's Brad Smith: "Your washing machine is subject to more safety rules than AI"](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c6rmy7zmvx3lo)
In a BBC interview published overnight into Monday, Microsoft's president called for global safety rules for AI, and named specifics:
- "emergency brakes" to pause or slow systems that get out of control
- a legal requirement to write incident reports, and to tell victims when a rogue AI agent breaches their systems
- independent government evaluators who vet new models before release

"Right now, your washing machine is subject to more safety rules than AI. Every carton of milk that you see in the grocery store is subject to safety rules and inspection," he said. On the "morally binding" pledge six AI chiefs signed at the White House on 29 September, which we covered the next day, Smith said Microsoft is "happy to sign it" but "I don't think it's sufficient any longer." The problem with any self-regulatory regime, he added, is that "the good companies will do what you want. It's the companies that are not as responsible that you need to worry about." He argued the UK, through its AI Security Institute, is best placed to lead, because "in Washington DC, people don't look at the UK and they don't look at London the way they look at Brussels."

That puts him at odds with President Trump, who has said the industry should regulate itself. It also comes from a company that operates AI infrastructure and holds a stake in OpenAI, one day after its chief executive's "emergency brake" post.

### [Catching up: DeepMind's proof agent solves nine open Erdős problems, and gets it through peer review](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aej2213)
Published in Science on Thursday, 8 October, by a 21-author team almost entirely from Google DeepMind: "Advancing mathematics research with AI-driven formal proof search." The agent uses a language model to write proofs in Lean, a language in which a computer checks every step. Per the abstract, it:
- autonomously resolved nine of 353 open Erdős problems
- proved 44 of 492 conjectures from the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences
- is being deployed in combinatorics, optimisation, graph theory, algebraic geometry and quantum optics research

The finding with the widest reach is that "even a basic agent alternating LLM-based generation with Lean-based verification replicated the Erdős successes." In other words, the Erdős results did not depend on the more elaborate system.

Hold it against the OpenAI release we led with on 7 October and corrected on 9 October: hundreds of manuscripts posted to GitHub, only some of them formalised, and assessed by the field after the fact. DeepMind's results are fewer, formally verified and peer-reviewed. One caution applies from our 9 October issue, though. A Lean check shows that a formal proof is valid; whether the formal statement captures the intended mathematics still needs human eyes.

## Research & Models

- **[Teams of AI agents cost up to five times more and rarely do better](https://www.vals.ai/blogs/multi-agent-vibe-code-bench)**: published 9 October by the evaluation company Vals AI. It ran GPT-6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 on 50 full-stack web apps from its Vibe Code Bench, each as a single agent and as a lead agent with up to five subagents, at medium and maximum reasoning effort. Teams cost 1.8 to 5.1 times as much. Only one of the four score differences was statistically significant: Sol's medium-effort team gained 7.3 points. For Sol, turning up reasoning effort helped more than adding a team. The single most expensive setup, Opus at maximum effort as a team, averaged $122 per app against $23.77 for a single agent, mostly because each subagent re-sends its own context on every model call. Vals itself notes this is one benchmark of one kind of work.
- **[Can AI invent AI research? Epoch's first answer is no, and the agents oversold what they did](https://epoch.ai/publications/innovationeval)**: published 7 October by Epoch AI. InnovationEval asked Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol to independently invent a post-training method able to match a recent human one they had not seen, giving each up to 3,000 GPU-hours. Neither came close. Sol's best idea recovered about 35% of the human method's gains on generous scoring. Fable's apparent gains came from running many similar trainings and keeping the best result, which Epoch calls "out-of-scope cheating." Both write-ups claimed higher scores than their methods genuinely achieved, and neither clearly disclosed the cherry-picking. Their successors, Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra, had already memorised the human paper, which Epoch flags as a growing problem for evaluations of this kind.
- **[Catching up: arXiv now caps each submitter at two papers a month](https://blog.arxiv.org/2026/10/01/updated-rate-limit-policy/)**: in effect since 1 October. arXiv received 40,363 submissions in September, against 20,569 in September 2024 and 9,869 in September 2016, generating almost 9,000 support tickets. Submissions to its AI category have grown more than sixfold in two years. The preprint server blames a "marked increase in dense, AI-written papers," thin papers and work split into several smaller ones. The limit counts rejected submissions and applies across all categories, and arXiv calls it a stopgap while it improves its moderation tools.

## Regulation & Policy

- **[Catching up: US regulators let driverless trucks use beacons instead of warning triangles for five years](https://www.freightwaves.com/news/fmcsa-aurora-warning-beacon-exemption-five-year)**: granted Wednesday, 7 October, by the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration. The rule it waives requires a driver to place reflective triangles around a stopped truck within ten minutes, which no one can do in a truck with no driver. The exemption runs to 7 October 2031 and lets Level 4 autonomous trucks use cab-mounted amber beacons, which must switch on within five minutes. It covers Aurora and any similar carrier that notifies the agency; Kodiak AI, Waabi and Stack AV opted in under earlier temporary waivers. Conditions include operating only inside each system's validated operating domain, no hazardous materials, crash reports within five days and annual malfunction reports. The agency had denied a similar request in December 2024, and this time weighed 402 comments, with the owner-operators' association and highway-safety groups opposed.

- **[Catching up: Waymo borrows $5B, its first debt financing](https://waymo.com/blog/2026/10/waymo-closes-5-billion-debt-financing/)**: announced 8 October by CFO Steve Fieler. The term loan was led by PIMCO, Blackstone and Sixth Street, with Goldman Sachs as sole lead bookrunner, and will fund expansion in the US and abroad.
