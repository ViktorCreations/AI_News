# AI News — Monday, September 14, 2026

> The slowdown consensus met its first real test today and lost three times over: AI stocks sold off across Asia, Europe and US premarket; Trump and the House Speaker rejected the idea outright; and Beijing called it fear mongering. The labs spent a fortnight agreeing that someone should slow them down, and Monday was the day everyone else answered.

## Top Stories

### [Markets put a price on the slowdown, and it is not small](https://edition.cnn.com/2026/09/14/business/ai-stocks-slide-slowdown-development-amodei-altman-intl)
AI-linked stocks fell worldwide on Monday, the first hard number anyone has attached to a fortnight of safety statements. Nasdaq futures dropped 1.5% on premarket declines in Nvidia and Intel. SoftBank, one of OpenAI's largest backers, closed nearly 11% lower in Tokyo. South Korea's Kospi sank 3.3%, dragged by a 6.4% fall in SK Hynix, and ASML lost 6% in Amsterdam. Neil Wilson, UK strategist at Saxo, wrote that analysts would be "scrabbling around to assess likely impact on earnings and valuations" if AI companies "coordinate a material slowing in development and erect guardrails" — which is the investment case stated in reverse. Landing the same morning, the Bank for International Settlements noted in its quarterly review that AI-led momentum in global equities has "wobbled" in recent months, with "rising concerns about the future profitability of significant AI investments and the sustainability of large profit margins... fuelled by the increasing leverage of major US tech firms." The pacing debate has been conducted so far as though it were free.

### [Trump, the Speaker and the White House AI chair all tell the labs no](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/13/nx-s1-5968078/trump-mike-johnson-ai-slowdown)
Speaking to reporters at his Doonbeg golf resort in Ireland on Sunday, Trump dismissed the warnings as exaggerated: "We're leading China in AI, we're the most sophisticated country in the world, and frankly, I want to keep it that way because whoever wins AI, wins. And we can put guardrails, we can do this and that. But I think you have a lot of negative forces that are bringing it up that shouldn't be bringing it up." He did not say who the negative forces are. House Speaker Mike Johnson, on CNN's State of the Union the same day, ruled out a moratorium because "China will overlap us" — "if Congress just races in and does some sort of emergency session to try to regulate AI, we will lose the race to China." The sharpest response came from David Sacks, co-chair of the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, who told the labs to go ahead without asking: "I don't see what you see in the lab. If the unreleased models are scary enough that you think you should slow down, I support your decision to be responsible" — but they should "stop pretending you need anyone else's permission," and "stop pretending the motivation to slow down is purely altruistic. You face massive product-liability exposure if your products enable a truly damaging cyberattack." That is the antitrust question from Friday answered from the other direction: no one is going to give them cover, and liability may be the real motive anyway.

### [Beijing calls it fear mongering, ten days before Trump meets Xi](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/14/nx-s1-5968456/china-hits-back-ai-development)
China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs responded on Monday to the part of Amodei's essay that got less attention when we covered it on Sunday: alongside the pacing argument, he warned that "a Chinese lead in AI would pose grave danger for the United States and the world" and called for continued restrictions on sales of cutting-edge chips and chipmaking equipment to China. "Fearmongering, confrontation and vicious competition will only disrupt the process of global AI governance which serves no one's interest," spokesperson Guo Jiakun said at Monday's regular press conference. A Global Times editorial on Sunday read the essay as a veiled call to "contain" China, and the Commerce Ministry separately dismissed the distillation allegations we covered on September 11 as groundless, saying distillation is common industry practice and the US is pursuing a "monopoly of the AI industry." Amodei's own essay concedes the bind: "global pacing will require cooperation with China, the autocratic country with by far the most advanced AI capabilities." Lizzi C. Lee of the Asia Society Policy Institute puts the contradiction plainly — "if the U.S. wants China to cooperate on frontier safety while also restricting its access to frontier compute, what exactly does that cooperation look like?" Trump and Xi are scheduled to meet on September 24, with AI governance on the agenda.

### [Altman turns "pacing" into a policy ask, and draws its limits](https://the-decoder.com/sam-altman-calls-for-pacing-ai-development-but-promises-rapid-progress-will-continue/)
Posting today, two days after Dario Amodei's essay and a day after endorsing part of it, Altman set out what he actually means. Pacing is not stopping: progress stays "rapid," just not faster than safety work can absorb. The concrete asks are a uniform, nationwide safety framework for advanced AI — one standard rather than a state patchwork — and US government backing for any international coordination, with the pointed caveat that the industry should not wait for lawmakers before acting. OpenAI, he says, has begun setting explicit safety criteria before major training runs. The line worth keeping is "no amount of American competitive pressure should justify recklessness," because the competitive-pressure argument is exactly what every previous version of this debate has ended on.

### [The obvious question about a fortnight of doom warnings](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/13/whats-behind-the-ai-industrys-latest-warnings-of-doom/)
TechCrunch's Equity team put the sceptical case on Sunday, and it deserves a hearing alongside the warnings themselves. Kirsten Korosec's reading is commercial: talking up how dangerous your technology is can be "a weird way of flexing" capability, and it lands with Anthropic's IPO approaching. Anthony Ha's is operational — the labs are saying this because they are "not really in control of these models anymore," which the month's agent incidents support. He is also blunt about the numbers: a stated ">10%" chance of catastrophe is a made-up figure rather than a calculation, and the apocalyptic register crowds out nearer harms like labour displacement and energy use. Both readings can be true at once, which is the uncomfortable part. Today's market reaction adds a third: the warnings are now expensive, which tests how sincerely they were meant.

## Research & Models

- **[Two open-weight search agents top their class](https://huggingface.co/collections/AllSpark-Research/iris)** — AllSpark released Iris-mini and Iris-pro on Sunday, agents that decide what to search for, read results and judge when they have enough evidence, built on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B respectively. Iris-mini scores 82.2 on BrowseComp, 84.8 on BrowseComp-ZH, 86.9 on DeepSearchQA and 52.3 on Humanity's Last Exam, leading three of the four among comparable open models; Iris-pro reaches 88.6, 85.1, 92.9 and 56.4. Weights are on Hugging Face with code on GitHub. The pattern of the past two months holds: the open-weight frontier for agentic work is being set on Chinese base models.

## Product & Industry

- **[ElevenLabs ships Music v2.5 and leans on its licensing](https://the-decoder.com/elevenlabs-makes-music-v2-5-available-via-app-and-api-with-free-and-pro-tier-options/)** — out Sunday through the ElevenMusic app and the API, with fuller and more natural output; in blind comparison across 47,885 track pairs listeners preferred v2.5, most strongly on R&B, soul, hip-hop, rock and orchestral. Free tier gives five lossless downloads a day with attribution, Pro gives 400 a month, and users keep ownership with commercial use depending on purpose. Downloads imitating a named artist are blocked, as is using someone else's song as a reference. The company says it trained on licensed stems and music — the same ground Suno moved onto last week when it rebuilt on Warner, BMG and Believe catalogue and retired its older models.

## Regulation & Policy

- **[Google, OpenAI and Anthropic have been quietly discussing their own standards body since July](https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2026/google-openai-and-anthropic-float-idea-of-ai-standards-body/)** — reported Sunday by The Information: representatives of the three have met regularly since July about creating an industry standards body, with the catalyst a July essay by DeepMind founder Demis Hassabis proposing a US-led body modelled on FINRA that would test advanced models before deployment. Altman told an OpenAI town hall he favours a testing and auditing organisation but expects the labs will have to build it themselves, without US government backing — which, read against Sacks telling them to stop asking permission, is now the likelier path. The talks predate Amodei's weekend essay, so the fortnight of public statements was tracking a private process rather than starting one.

## AI in Real Estate

- **[All but one landlord has now settled the DOJ's algorithmic rent-pricing case](https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/doj-pinnacle-settlement-realpage-lawsuit/830108/)** — the Justice Department filed a proposed consent decree with Pinnacle Property Management Services on September 4, announced September 10, in *United States et al. v. RealPage et al.* in the Middle District of North Carolina. That leaves Camden Property Trust as the only defendant still fighting the government, after RealPage, Cortland, Greystar, Blackstone's LivCor and Willow Bridge settled earlier. This is the DOJ's own case, not the private class action in the Middle District of Tennessee we covered on Thursday — Camden settled that one for $53M in May, and is a holdout only here. Pinnacle — a Cushman & Wakefield subsidiary since 2020 — admits no fault but must stop using any pricing algorithm that generates recommendations from competitors' confidential data, appoint a chief antitrust compliance officer, train every employee annually, and accept a court-appointed monitor if it uses an uncertified third-party pricing tool. Set against RealPage's August launch of an agent suite reaching much further into operations than pricing ever did, which we covered on Saturday, the remedy is worth reading closely: it does not ban algorithmic pricing, it bans algorithmic pricing fed by rivals' data. The industry's response is already being built on that distinction.
