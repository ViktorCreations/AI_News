# AI News — Saturday, October 3, 2026

> Apple is rebuilding a macOS permission because of AI agents, which is what it looks like when agents stop being a product category and start being a threat model the platform has to design around. Meanwhile OpenAI has agreed to put a frontier model inside Synopsys's chip-design tools on a shared-revenue basis, and Amazon has answered the data-centre backlash with a billion dollars and a written list of promises.

## Top Stories

### [Apple is tightening Full Disk Access because of AI agents](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/02/apple-says-its-tightening-macos-full-disk-access-controls-due-to-new-risks-from-ai-agents/)
Announced Friday. Full Disk Access is the macOS permission that hands an app everything on the machine, and Apple says it will add new controls requiring "very explicit user action" before it is granted, so that users "clearly understand these risks." The reasoning is stated plainly and is the reason this matters: "Addressing this is critical. As AI agents become increasingly capable and autonomous, the risks associated with this level of access will grow substantially." Apple also says some developers are already using the permission "in ways that could put users at risk, exposing everything on their systems… without users' full knowledge and understanding." It follows a journalist's claim that Meta's Muse app read private messages without permission — which Meta disputes — and a reported flaw in ChatGPT's Mac app. Apple has not said which macOS version carries the change or when. Note the shift in posture: the agent era's security problem is being treated not as something to warn users about but as something to take out of their hands.

### [OpenAI and Synopsys will build a model that drives chip-design tools, and split the revenue](https://investor.synopsys.com/news/news-details/2026/OpenAI-and-Synopsys-Announce-GPT-Synopsys-Frontier-Intelligence-to-Revolutionize-Chip-Design/)
Announced on 30 September at Synopsys's Investor Day, and missed in our last two issues. GPT-Synopsys is a specialised model, under a multi-year agreement, trained to reason about semiconductor design and operate Synopsys's electronic design automation tools directly: an engineer sets an objective — power, performance and area optimisation, timing closure, verification closure — and agents run the tools, read the results, change the design and iterate. OpenAI licenses Synopsys's EDA tools to build it; the two will sell compute, model and licences as a joint offering under a "shared revenue framework," with the model running on OpenAI-hosted infrastructure and integrated into Synopsys.ai and Synopsys Autopilot. Customer design data is not used for training and is encrypted in transit and at rest, with configurable retention, audit and permissions. Greg Brockman's line is the strategic one — "By helping them build better chips, we can build better AI" — and it describes a loop the industry has been talking about for two years and has rarely put under contract. Early engagements with semiconductor customers are said to be underway; there is no general availability date.

### [Meta open-sources the hardware side of Muse](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/02/meta-wants-you-to-build-your-own-muse-gadget/)
Published Friday: Meta has released Muse Gadgets, an open-source project comprising firmware and a Linux SDK for building your own hardware front-end to Muse, its personal agent that books travel, fills in forms and shops on a user's behalf. The suggested builds are deliberately modest — e-ink displays, HDMI sticks, Raspberry Pi and ESP32 boards — and the code is published at gadgets.muse.ai. Alongside it Meta made Muse Home Link, a USB-C device that connects the agent to home networks and smart devices, and manufactured 5,000 of them to give away to subscribers. Nat Friedman, head of product at Meta's Superintelligence Labs, posted about Home Link on X. Read it next to the Apple item above: one company is making it easier to give an agent a physical foothold in the house while another is making it harder to give one the contents of a disk.

## Research & Models

- **[Google moved federated learning onto trusted hardware, and shipped it in Gboard](https://research.google/blog/toward-provably-private-learning-from-federated-data/)** — published Friday by Katharine Daly and Daniel Ramage, with a paper at [arXiv:2609.31494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31494). The change is where the computation happens: instead of keeping training on devices, this system moves it server-side into trusted execution environments and tries to make the privacy claim externally checkable rather than merely asserted — access policies published to the Rekor transparency log, reproducible builds from the open-source Confidential Federated Compute repository, and training logic expressed as Python programs whose execution can be verified, on top of differential privacy. It is already running on Gboard's English and Japanese next-word prediction models, where server-side parallelisation cut training cycles that took one to two months. The authors are candid that current trusted-hardware generations are limited and that side-channel research is unfinished, which is the right caveat: verifiability here rests on the chip keeping its promises.

## Product & Industry

- **[Anthropic will spend $100M training 10,000 engineers to deploy Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-frontier-academy)** — announced Friday: Claude Frontier Academy is explicitly modelled on medical residency — multi-day in-person training with Anthropic engineers in San Francisco, New York and London, then a 12-week residency running a real Claude project back at the engineer's own employer, with a "Claude Resident Engineer" badge after the first part and "Frontier Deployed Engineer" after the second. Entry is by nomination, and the engineer is expected to arrive with a named project to lead. The target is 10,000 such engineers by the end of 2027; first cohorts include Accenture, Bain, Capgemini, Commonwealth Bank of Australia, Deloitte, McKinsey, Morgan Stanley and Novo Nordisk, with first graduates expected in early 2027. The bottleneck being addressed is not model capability but the supply of people who can put one into production.
- **[Sean Parker is rebuilding Stability AI around music](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/02/sean-parker-is-rebuilding-stability-ai-around-music/)** — reported Friday: the company Parker helped rescue two years ago with an $80M investment, now run by chief executive Prem Akkaraju, is repositioning from image generation to being the toolmaker for music professionals, with three new audio models, music-editing software, and an update that will let a user hum a melody or beatbox a drum pattern to steer the model. We covered the money on 26 August — $76M from Universal, Sony and Warner — and this is what it bought: the labels licensed their catalogues for training as part of the deal, and the company is now building for the industry that spent three years suing companies like it. Parker's framing of the contrast with his Napster years is that this time he is "playing by the rules" — with the labels' approval, and their money.

## Compute & Data Centers

- **[Amazon answers the data-centre backlash with $1B and a written commitment](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-data-centers-built-together)** — announced Friday by AWS chief executive Matt Garman: "Built Together" adds more than $1B over five years in the US communities where Amazon builds and runs data centres, across three pillars — free certificates and associate degrees in trades and other fields, targeting 300,000 students and up to 100,000 workers a year through modular training centres by the end of 2028; energy and water work covering more than 30,000 homes and 300-plus schools and community buildings, with around $700 a year in household savings claimed, plus 65-plus water projects returning more than 8 billion gallons a year; and flexible funding that communities themselves direct to roads, parks, housing or food security. The separate Amazon Data Center Commitment is the more unusual document, because it gives up things: no more non-disclosure agreements with government agencies, EPA Tier 4 backup generators at new sites, annual public reporting of energy and water use and carbon-free energy share, and electricity prices set to cover the infrastructure its own sites require so local bills do not rise. Garman concedes the premise — "There are clearly areas in which the technology and energy industries can be doing better" — which is a notable thing for the largest builder in the sector to put in writing. He also makes the argument the money is really for, warning against more than 100 data-centre moratoriums now under consideration across the country: "If these measures are enacted, the U.S. could be writing its own losing ticket to this race, and the consequences would last generations." Keep the proportions in view. Amazon said in July it expects $220B of capital expenditure in 2026 alone; this is $1B over five years, and the Associated Press notes that about six in ten Americans, across both parties, support limiting how many new data centres get built.

## Regulation & Policy

- **[The US arrests a California executive over $300M of smuggled Nvidia servers](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/california-man-arrested-smuggling-more-300-million-export-controlled-computer-servers-china)** — announced Thursday: Greg Lui, 38, of San Gabriel, owner of Earthmade Computer Inc., was arrested on a three-count indictment charging conspiracy to violate the Export Control Reform Act, outbound smuggling, and conspiracy to commit money laundering — carrying maximum terms of 20, 10 and 20 years. Prosecutors allege that between 2023 and 2024 Lui and co-conspirators gave US manufacturers false paperwork about end users and destinations, shipped export-controlled GPU servers to Malaysia and Singapore where no licence is required, then re-exported them to China; Earthmade is said to have received more than $176M from Malaysian shipping companies between January and October 2024. Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security, the Defense Criminal Investigative Service and the FBI investigated. One detail worth noting for anyone tracking last week's renaming order: the quotes from the Justice Department's own officials now say "SI," not AI — "SI is the defining technology of the era," said Assistant Attorney General John A. Eisenberg.
- **[A new federal rule on government data inside LLMs takes effect on 19 October](https://nextgov.com/acquisition/2026/10/gsa-memo-set-ai-specific-acquisition-rules/416378)** — reported Thursday: the General Services Administration has approved a section of its acquisition regulation titled "Basic Safeguarding of Data within Large Language Model Artificial Intelligence Systems," signed by senior procurement executive Jeffrey Koses as part of memo RGO-2026-01. It bars contractors from using government data to train LLMs, to inform advertising, or to sell to third parties, and requires encrypted transmission and audit logging; one practitioner quoted describes a 120-day disclosure deadline, 72-hour incident reporting, and deletion of embeddings and fine-tuned weights at contract closeout. It does not reach internal business-use LLMs or products where the LLM is incidental. GSA dropped contested "unbiased AI principles" language after industry pushback, Palantir's included — so what survives is the data-handling part, which is also the enforceable part.

## AI in Real Estate

- **[RealPage wins a pause on New York's algorithmic rent-setting ban](https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/realpage-injunction-new-york-algorithmic-rent-setting-ban/832045/)** — on 29 September Judge Valerie Caproni of the Southern District of New York granted RealPage a preliminary injunction against the state law, in force since December 2025, that bars facilitating agreements between landlords not to compete on rents "including by operating or licensing a software, data analytics service, or algorithmic device" — and denied the attorney general's January motion to dismiss. The court held that algorithmically-informed price recommendations are commercial speech, and that "as written, under the rubric of preventing price fixing, the Statute prohibits normal commercial conduct just because it is facilitated by software," while calling its own merits finding "a close call." Landlords may keep using the software while the case proceeds. We have covered the settlement side of this fight repeatedly — the DOJ consent decrees, the DC penalties — and this is the first ruling that goes the other way: the First Amendment argument against banning pricing algorithms has now survived a motion to dismiss.
- **[What multifamily operators say they actually use AI for](https://www.multifamilydive.com/news/artificial-intelligence-property-management-due-diligence/831656/)** — reported 29 September, and useful precisely because it is about deployed systems rather than launches. Security Properties built its own underwriting tool, FirstPass, which chief executive Dan Byrnes says takes a baseline deal model from a week to five minutes — "that has only enhanced our ability to really look at everything everywhere." Bonaventure runs a "company brain" over meeting transcripts and a property-performance dashboard, and uses it to surface HUD-loan acquisition opportunities. Camden Property Trust applies predictive analytics to insurance claims and equipment-replacement timing. All three draw the same line at headcount, and Camden's Alex Jessett puts the limit bluntly: the business is "high trust, high touch."
