# AI News — Tuesday, August 18, 2026

> Three stories today are the same story: the hunt for text nobody else has. An Israeli agency stood up a fake think tank to feed chatbots, Amazon is slicing the spines off rare books to scan them, and Google bought a dead airline's inbox at a bankruptcy auction. A paper out today argues you will not be able to prove any of it later.

## Top Stories

### [An Israeli government contractor built a fake think tank to feed AI chatbots](https://responsiblestatecraft.org/israel-influence-chatgpt/)
The Hanover Institute for Public Policy launched on August 6 and published more than 100 reports in about a week — none with bylines, and a small disclaimer noting it was created by Piro, Inc. for the Israeli Government Advertising Agency. Responsible Statecraft's Nick Cleveland-Stout reports FARA filings showing $900,000 from the Israeli government, and GPTZero flagged 11 of 12 randomly sampled articles as high-confidence AI-written. The target is not readers. Piro advertises "AI Story Optimization" — content shaped for how language models weigh credibility — and the reports are built in formulaic structures that answer the kind of question a chatbot gets asked. NewsGuard's Alice Lee notes the site borrows the visual grammar of a real institute. The technique has a name now, LLM poisoning, and this is what it looks like with a state budget behind it.

### [Amazon is cutting up rare books to scan them for training data](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/17/amazon-once-an-online-bookseller-is-destroying-rare-books-to-train-ai-models/)
404 Media hid a tracking device in a rare book and followed it to VGT3, an Amazon facility in Las Vegas, where books bought through commercial channels have their spines cut off and are scanned. Amazon's response is that it "purchases books through commercial channels to improve the products and services customers use." The economics are the point: text written and printed before 2022 is now a scarce input, because it is guaranteed not to be model output, and training on model output degrades the model. A company that began by selling books is destroying them for the words inside.

### [Google bought a bankrupt airline's data at auction for $10 million](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/18/google-buys-crashed-airline-spirits-data-at-auction-because-ai/5288962)
Per a court filing, the bankruptcy liquidation of Spirit Airlines included a data lot Google took for $10M: 100 million emails, 500 million Teams items, 30 million recorded customer service calls, 15 million service chats, 17 million OneDrive files, 600,000 ServiceNow tickets, 13.7 million email addresses from Oracle's Responsys, and operational records on 763,000 flights down to fuel slips and crew pairings. Google says it will use the data to improve its AI services, and the filing says the material was deidentified before sale, with Google committing to scrub any personal information it finds. Whether deidentification survives contact with 30 million recorded phone calls is the open question — and a bankruptcy estate is now a channel through which a company's private correspondence becomes someone else's training corpus.

## Research & Models

- **[MIT: you often cannot prove which training data produced an output](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/18/ai-models-get-convenient-amnesia-about-source-material-as-they-grow-mit-boffins-find/5288846)** — Zheng Dai and David Gifford of MIT CSAIL publish "Outputs of Generative Diffusion Models are Often Unattributable" in *Nature Communications* today. Using ablation — removing specific training data and retraining — they find what they call attribution decay: as models get larger, cutting out the Mona Lisa, or all of Leonardo's work, stops preventing the model from reproducing the image or the style. The implication runs straight into the copyright docket, where attribution is how you connect a specific work to a specific output, but the authors are careful that the same problem afflicts machine unlearning, interpretability, privacy and data-poisoning defence. Scale does not just make provenance hard to trace; it makes the question ill-posed.

## Product & Industry

- **[An autonomous security agent took Snowflake's Jira credentials through a GitHub Actions bug](https://www.wiz.io/blog/red-agent-snowflake-copilot-cicd-bug)** — Wiz's writeup surfaced on Monday: its "Red Agent" found and exploited a script-injection flaw in a `jira_issue.yml` workflow in a public Snowflake repo on June 23, five days after the workflow went live, by crafting an issue title. It got Snowflake's internal Jira token and email, with read access to engineering, security-compliance and bug-bounty projects. Snowflake patched the same day, rotated credentials and found no third-party access. Worth correcting a misreading doing the rounds: Copilot Autofix did not write the bug — it reviewed the pull request containing it and marked it all-clear, having contributed a fix to a different file in the same PR. The failure is the review, not the generation.
- **[Gartner expects agentic AI to cost five times more by 2028](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/17/agentic-ai-costs-set-to-balloon-fivefold-by-2028/5288363)** — senior director analyst Will Sommer calls it the inference paradox: per-token prices keep falling, and total spend keeps rising, because an agent does not answer once. Where "a simple chatbot must read and interpret a query and quickly respond," an agent "must constantly reason, negotiate, and question itself." Cheaper tokens fund more elaborate workflows, and consumption outruns the discount. It sits oddly beside Anthropic's own advice last week on session hygiene — the same arithmetic, seen from the buyer's end.

## Compute & Data Centers

- **[Nvidia takes an equity stake in the developer of OpenAI's Ohio campus](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/17/nvidia-investing-1-5b-in-softbank-data-center-developer-behind-openai-project/)** — announced Monday, three days after the WSJ reported Nvidia had cut its guarantee on this project from $250B to under $120B: it will invest $1.5B in SB Energy, the SoftBank-backed developer, and extend up to $105B of credit for construction. The site is the PORTS-Pike campus in Pike County, scaling from 4.25GW to a possible 8GW on a 20-year lease to OpenAI, alongside a 9.2GW natural gas plant on Department of Energy land — a former uranium enrichment site — at an estimated $33B, plus at least $4.2B into regional grid infrastructure. The arrangement makes Nvidia the sole compute supplier at the campus. Jensen Huang used Monday's post to argue this is not circular investing; the structure is a chip vendor funding the buyer of its chips and taking a stake in the landlord.
