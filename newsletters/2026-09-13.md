# AI News — Sunday, September 13, 2026

> Dario Amodei published a plan to slow the frontier — embedded auditors, shared standards, and eventually a treaty-level speed limit on recursive self-improvement — and Altman, Musk and Hassabis each backed at least part of it. Meanwhile Nvidia is reportedly in talks to anchor Anthropic's IPO with up to $10B, which is the same week's other answer to the question of how fast this should go.

## Top Stories

### [Amodei sets out how to slow the frontier, and his rivals partly agree](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier)
"We Must Pace the Frontier," published Saturday, is the most concrete version of the slowdown argument anyone at a frontier lab has put in writing. It proposes three tiers. First, a unilateral commitment Anthropic could make alone: third-party evaluators embedded with employee-level access — desks, badges, internal systems — and the right to publish what they find. Second, common safety standards among democratic countries' labs, which Amodei concedes may need government cover to survive antitrust. Third, international negotiation in four escalating steps: ban clearly dangerous uses such as bioweapons, require pre-release testing for acute risks, impose a speed limit on recursive self-improvement, and only then contemplate a general pause. The urgency claim is specific and checkable: he argues AI swarms could threaten internet infrastructure within six to twelve months, and frames the next three to five years as the window in which democracies keep their lead. By Sunday, Sam Altman, Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis had each endorsed part of it on X, converging in particular on independent evaluation of labs. That is four of the people who set the pace agreeing in public that someone should be allowed to check their work — and, as OpenAI's approach to Congress about Sherman Act exposure showed on Friday, agreeing is the easy part.

### [A two-year randomised trial found banning AI left students worst off](https://the-decoder.com/two-year-university-study-finds-banning-ai-from-classrooms-leaves-students-worse-off/)
Thibault Schrepel ran a controlled experiment across two cohorts of his Law of AI course at Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam — 66 students in 2024, 164 in 2025 — splitting them into a group barred from AI, a group given AI with no instruction, and a group trained in how to use it. The no-AI group came last both years. The untrained group edged ahead of them despite making uncritical errors with the tool. The trained group led in 2024, but by 2025 its advantage had nearly vanished, which is its own finding about how quickly a taught skill becomes ambient. The detail most likely to survive: the no-AI group hit "idea exhaustion" after ten to fifteen minutes on classroom exercises. Schrepel's summary of his own prior position is "I was wrong"; the paper is on SSRN as a preprint. Worth setting against New York City's decision, which we covered on Tuesday, to bar generative AI for 600,000 students through eighth grade — different ages and a different purpose, but the same underlying bet.

### [Astra can run a year-long business but has a 2.8% chance of flying a drone](https://the-decoder.com/gpt-6-astra-pilots-a-surveillance-drone-and-runs-a-business-on-its-own/)
Andon Labs put GPT-6 Astra through two evaluations, and the gap between them is the story. On Vending-Bench, a simulated year running a vending machine business from $500 — negotiating with suppliers, buying inventory, setting prices — Astra finished with an average bank balance of $15,515 against Claude Fable 5.1's $5,422, and its *worst* run beat Fable's best. For scale against our own archive: when we covered this benchmark on July 30, the record holder was Claude Opus 5 at a mean $11,182, a mark Astra has now cleared by a third. On Drone-Bench, where models write code to make a DJI Tello navigate an office, find a specific person and follow them, it managed 40% on person detection and 10% on 3D reconstruction, giving it roughly a 2.8% chance of completing all five tasks in sequence. Long-horizon commercial reasoning in a simulator is now strong; grounding that reasoning in physical space is not close. The surveillance framing in the headline is doing work the results do not support.

## Capital & Deals

- **[Nvidia is reportedly in talks to anchor Anthropic's IPO with up to $10B](https://the-decoder.com/nvidia-wants-to-pour-up-to-10-billion-into-anthropics-record-breaking-ipo/)** — per Reuters on Friday: Anthropic is targeting roughly $100B raised at a valuation near $2 trillion, which would be the largest IPO ever, and Nvidia is discussing up to $10B as an anchor investor to secure stock before trading opens. Nothing is signed. The circularity is worth naming plainly — Anthropic runs on Nvidia silicon, and Nvidia would be buying into the company buying its chips, a pattern now visible across several of the year's largest AI transactions.
- **[Altman says an OpenAI listing this year would be "ill-advised"](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/12/openais-sam-altman-says-it-would-be-ill-advised-to-go-public-in-2026/)** — said Saturday, and the contrast with the item above is the point: the two leading labs are taking opposite routes to the same problem of funding compute at this scale, one heading for public markets at a record valuation and the other explicitly staying out of them for now.

## Quick Hits

- [A satire of the slowdown discourse did numbers this weekend](https://xeiaso.net/notes/2026/everyone-slowdown-but-me/) — Xe Iaso's Saturday piece calls for a global pause on frontier research with one exception, their own fictional company, and admits the motivation is "the number of leading zeroes in Techaro's bank account." It is a joke with a thesis: every pause proposal is also a competitive position, which is precisely the objection the embedded-auditor part of Amodei's plan is designed to answer.

## AI in Real Estate

- **[Proptech money is going to the back office, not the shop window](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/software-and-technology/proptech-money-is-clustering-around-ops-automation-not-new-listing-sites)** — an August 31 read of the funding data: of roughly $275.4M disclosed across 12 deals through July, about $212.5M — 77% — went to property and rental operations rather than listing or marketplace plays, with a median equity round near $5M once one outsized deal is excluded. The representative cheque is New York's Uniti, which raised a $12M Series A led by Pathlight with MetaProp to put AI agents on tour scheduling, inquiry follow-up, phone handling, collections, maintenance and payments, citing Regus, StorQuest, RHP Properties and Storage King USA as customers. Its published ROI and retention figures are company-supplied case studies, so treat them accordingly. The pattern matters more than any single round: investors have stopped funding new ways to find a home and started funding the removal of the people who administer one.
