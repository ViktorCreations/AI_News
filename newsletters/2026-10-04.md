# AI News — Sunday, October 4, 2026

> The man who wrote OpenAI's safety reports has quit and says the industry needs to run like a nuclear plant rather than a startup — the latest in a run of safety departures that began with Jacob Coxon on 8 September, and the first by the person who wrote the documents that accompanied each launch. The weekend's other news is the machinery assembling around that argument: a bipartisan Senate bill to make AI companies criminally liable when their agents hack something, and a federal judge calling a nationwide automated surveillance network unconstitutional.

## Top Stories

### [OpenAI's safety-report author quits and says the culture is broken](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/03/openai-safety-employee-resigns-claiming-the-companys-culture-is-broken/)
Reported Saturday. David Robinson spent three and a half years at OpenAI — among its longest-tenured employees — writing the safety reports that accompanied each major model release, and has resigned and published an essay in *The Atlantic* saying the company's "culture is broken." His diagnosis is specific rather than atmospheric: OpenAI "thrives by trial and error (which it calls 'iterative deployment')," a method that "by its very nature, guarantees periodic failures," run by an industry of "extreme confidence" and "perpetual sprints." The remedy he proposes is borrowed expertise — he says he "never encountered a colleague who had experience making airplanes fly safely or nuclear reactors" — and the standard he names, in the passage [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1004408/openai-safety-quits-sounding-the-alarm) quotes from the essay, is explicit: "Given today's risks, frontier labs need to run like nuclear power plants or busy airports, with layers of redundancy and careful, time-consuming planning, so that the occasional and inevitable human error does not open a door to disaster." He cites the Hugging Face breach by OpenAI agents and the continuing discovery of rogue agents. OpenAI's spokesperson Drew Pusateri says the company is "making sure our models don't become more capable than we can safely manage." Two things make this more than another resignation letter. The run is now a pattern — Jacob Coxon left both OpenAI and Anthropic on 8 September saying they were "gambling with our lives," and The Verge counts Robert O'Callahan, Bilal Chughtai and Josh Engels at Google DeepMind and Joe Benton at Anthropic since. And Robinson is not a critic who joined to object: his job was to produce the document that said a launch was acceptable.

### [Capcom plans to turn its game engine into an "AI-generation" engine](https://www.theverge.com/games/1004418/capcom-ai-game-development)
Published Saturday, and more consequential than a studio saying it likes AI. At the Capcom Open Conference RE: 2026, programmer Satoshi Ishida presented on the future of RE Engine — the technology behind *Resident Evil* — and argued that the only answer to the time cost of building games at that scale is "successfully integrating AI technology into development workflows." The plan he described is incremental: evolve RE Engine into an "AI-generation game engine," working toward "a future where we create games together with AI," per IGN's translation. The constraint Capcom has stated before is that it will not use AI-generated assets in shipped games, confining the technology to the pipeline rather than the product — a line that the engine roadmap leaves room to move. Worth noting what is absent from both the talk and the coverage: any commitment about headcount.

## Research & Models

- **[Catching up: a 7B open-weights robot policy took first place on RoboLab, and the cost comparison is the story](https://bfl.ai/blog/flux-3-action)** — published by Black Forest Labs on 23 September and missed at the time. FLUX 3 Action is a 7-billion-parameter world action model, released with open weights, which predicts video and actions jointly rather than separating them. Its single-step checkpoint beats every other open policy on RoboLab at 38.3% ± 0.38, against 36.8% for the 16B Cosmos 3 Nano and 28.0% for π0.5, and a guidance-distilled checkpoint raises that to 42.2% ± 0.36 — while running 1.34x to 2.28x faster per second of robot motion than π0.5. The arresting numbers are economic. On the benchmark of Su et al., GPT-6 Astra at maximum reasoning effort solves every task, but costs $13.47 and 16 minutes per success and spends about 35.77 seconds of compute per second of robot motion; F3A costs $0.09 per success and 21 milliseconds per second of motion, and cannot solve every task. Hand the reasoner the job of delegating to F3A and stepping in only when needed, and the hybrid solves 90% of episodes at $8.77 and eight minutes — 29% cheaper and 40% faster than the best alternative. Black Forest Labs is candid that pure reasoning still scores highest, so the reliability-versus-cost trade-off survives; what moves is where it sits.

## Product & Industry

- **[Anthropic starts asking Claude users to donate their voice recordings](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/artificial-intelligence/anthropic-asks-claude-users-to-share-voice-data-for-ai-model-training/)** — spotted Sunday: a new prompt in Claude's voice features asks users to "allow us to use your voice data to improve our AI models," explaining that "audio recordings and voice chat data help improve how Anthropic AI models understand and respond to speech." The details that matter are the right ones: it is off by default, declining is one tap, it lives as its own toggle under Settings → Privacy, and it is separate from the existing permission covering chats and Claude Code sessions — so a user can share voice while withholding text, or the reverse. Anthropic says the data can be deleted later from settings. An opt-in with a default of off is the version of this that does not need defending, which is worth saying plainly given how rarely it is the version that ships.
- **[A two-person startup is red-teaming chatbots with simulated vulnerable users](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/02/circuit-breaker-labs-hopes-to-make-ai-safer-for-your-kids-and-you/)** — reported Friday: Circuit Breaker Labs, founded by siblings Shirali Nigam (chief executive) and Arul Nigam (chief technology officer) and still at five employees, builds agents that imitate users of different ages, languages and cultures and runs tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of simulated conversations a day against a model, looking for psychological harm before deployment and producing what it calls auditable, explainable scores. The founders cite the death of Sewell Setzer, the 14-year-old who killed himself after conversations with Character.AI. The technical claim is narrow and plausible: harm hides in the gap between how people actually talk and what models were tuned on. "Models are really good at handling standard speech patterns, but nobody actually talks like that," Nigam says. Customers are unnamed and funding undisclosed.

## Regulation & Policy

- **[Hawley and Murphy want prison time when an AI agent hacks something](https://www.hawley.senate.gov/senators-hawley-murphy-announce-bipartisan-ai-agent-accountability-act/)** — announced Thursday, and still an announcement rather than an introduced bill: the AI Agent Accountability Act would extend the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act to reach both ends of the supply chain. Operators would face civil and criminal liability for knowingly running an agent that recklessly causes hacking damage; developers would face it for failing to put reasonable safeguards in place when they knew or should have known an agent could be used to hack. The Attorney General and state attorneys general could sue to enjoin either. "These AI agents are committing cyberattacks," Hawley says. "If Big Tech companies are going to design AI agents that wreak havoc, these companies better be on the hook for any damage that is caused." Murphy's framing is the same from the other direction: "Hacking is a crime, and when AI agents conduct dangerous cyberattacks, the corporations and executives responsible need accountability." Note what has changed in a fortnight: the question is no longer whether agent misbehaviour is a policy problem but which body of law absorbs it — the FTC reached for consumer protection on 30 September, and this reaches for computer-crime law.
- **[A federal judge calls automated plate-reader networks "indiscriminate mass surveillance"](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/03/federal-judge-calls-flock-indiscriminate-mass-surveillance/)** — reported Saturday: Judge Sara Hill, in a Tulsa case, held that a sheriff's deputy violated the Fourth Amendment by searching Flock Safety's database without a warrant, and ordered all evidence obtained after that search suppressed as "the fruit of a poisonous tree." Her reasoning is the part with reach beyond this defendant: warrantless tracking becomes "constitutionally problematic when law enforcement can indiscriminately and passively catalog your whereabouts," which she describes as "a type of indiscriminate mass surveillance." It lands as the political pressure converges — on Friday Bernie Sanders, with Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Jeff Merkley, introduced the Ban Flock Act, which would bar federal agencies from using plate readers or the data from those run by police and private companies, and which TechCrunch notes faces long odds. We covered Flock's chief executive asking for "compromise" on 24 August, as jurisdictions began cancelling contracts; the courts have now answered before Congress has.

## Capital & Deals

- **[CScale leaves stealth with $145M, and Nvidia and Intel as its first strategic investors](https://www.cscale.ai/press/cscale-exits-stealth)** — announced 30 September: a $145M Series C led by Atreides Management with Valor Equity Partners and Premji Invest co-leading, plus Sutter Hill Ventures and Maverick Silicon, taking the Palo Alto company founded in 2023 to $188M raised. What it builds is unglamorous and increasingly load-bearing: an integrated light engine for optical interconnect between accelerators, designed so that a laser failure is contained rather than halting the compute around it. "As AI scale-up domains extend across dozens of racks, optical interconnect becomes essential," says chief executive Martin Lund. Nvidia and Intel Capital joining as first strategic investors is the signal to read — the people selling the accelerators are funding the wiring between them.
- **[Instinct's personal agent is approaching $1B of annual transactions, mostly travel](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/29/instinct-founder-said-more-than-50-of-transactions-on-the-platform-are-travel-related/)** — the round was announced on 28 September: $1B at a $10B valuation from Sequoia, Benchmark and Coatue, four times the $2.5B mark the 14-person company carried a month earlier. The operating numbers arrived a day later, from founder Noah Shinn on Patrick O'Shaughnessy's podcast: more than half of transactions on the platform are travel-related, the platform is growing around 10% a day with transaction volume keeping pace, and it is "approaching a billion dollars in annual transactions." Shinn's explanation for the travel concentration is the clearest statement of what consumer agents currently replace: "people who are not on Instinct use travel agencies (or sites) to book hotels or flights." User numbers remain undisclosed.
